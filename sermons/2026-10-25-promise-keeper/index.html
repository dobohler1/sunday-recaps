What it was about

Dr. Godfrey's doctoral work was on trauma-informed preaching, and this sermon was one. She opened by asking everyone to answer silently: have you ever experienced trauma? Her point was that trauma is in the pews every Sunday, that the church mostly skips past it, and that a lot of what people carry was never theirs to begin with. It was handed down from parents, grandparents, and generations before that. For Black men especially, she named the father wound: growing up without a father in the house, or with one who never said "I'm proud of you."

Then she went to the three men God names when He introduces Himself to Moses: "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." She read their family story as a case study in how hurt travels from one generation to the next.

  • Abraham left everything familiar, got afraid in a foreign land, and lied about his wife Sarah to protect himself. Later he climbed Mount Moriah to sacrifice his son Isaac. God stopped him, but the text never shows father and son speaking again.
  • Isaac, the boy on the altar, grew up and told the same lie about his own wife, in almost the same words. He played favorites between his twin sons.
  • Jacob was raised on favoritism and learned to take: his brother's birthright, then his brother's blessing by disguising himself before his blind father. He was deceived in turn by his father-in-law, then by his own sons, who faked Joseph's death with goat's blood after he had fooled his father with a goat.
  • Joseph's generation is where it turns. Judah, one of the overlooked sons, offers himself in place of the youngest brother. The pattern stops with him.

Her four points

  • How trauma travels. Nobody in that family set out to teach lying, favoritism, or silence. It got passed down anyway, through silence, through survival habits, through unequal love, and through what a father never received and so couldn't give. "Some of us are not crazy. We're carrying something that was handed down to us."
  • Transformation through faith and reconciliation. Jacob wrestles with God and gets a new name, then faces the brother he robbed, who runs to embrace him. Transformation begins when we stop wrestling with people and start wrestling with God. Forgiveness you can do alone; reconciliation takes two people telling the truth, and doesn't mean going back to someone who is still dangerous.
  • Healing and redemption. Where was God in all this? With Isaac at the well named "the God who sees me." With Jacob in the wilderness. With Joseph in the pit and the prison. God doesn't always prevent the trauma, but God refuses to leave you alone in it. Jesus rose with the scars still on Him. Redemption doesn't erase the scars; it means they stop being the end of the story.
  • What Joseph's family teaches us today. Eight practical lessons: name what happened; let yourself grieve; don't confuse being fruitful with forgetting; look for real change before restoring trust; be the one who breaks the pattern; let God reframe the story; speak kindly where there has been silence; and bless all your children.

She closed to the fathers in the room: it's not too late to say "I'm proud of you" or "I'm sorry," and Jesus and therapy are not enemies. To the sons and daughters: you may never get the apology you deserve, but you can still receive the blessing from a Father who never leaves. What was handed down to you doesn't have to be handed down through you.
