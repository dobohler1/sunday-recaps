Promise Keeper, For You

A short version of a sermon that was mostly aimed at grown men, rewritten for younger ones. About a five-minute read. Then three questions at the bottom.

The sermon was long. But at the center of it was a family story about lying, and that part is for you right now. This isn't a lecture. It's the story, and what it's actually about.

1The first lie was a scared lie

Abraham had to leave everything he knew and move to a strange country. When he got there he was afraid, and he told the king a lie about his wife Sarah to protect himself. The preacher called it "a survival reflex." Not a plan, not evil. Just a scared person grabbing the fastest way out.

That's worth sitting with, because that's how most lies work. Almost nobody lies because they enjoy it. People lie because they're afraid of what happens if the truth comes out. Afraid of getting in trouble, afraid of looking bad, afraid of letting someone down.

So the real question when you catch yourself lying is not "why am I a liar." It's "what was I afraid of?" That's a question you can actually do something about.

Under almost every lie is a fear. Find the fear.

2The lie never stays with the person who told it

Abraham's lie protected Abraham. It put Sarah in danger. She was the one who paid for it. The preacher said fear "spills onto the people closest to us," and that is exactly what a lie does.

Think about who ends up holding the bag when you lie. A brother who gets blamed. A parent who trusted you and now doesn't know what to trust. A friend who covered for you. The lie buys you an hour of safety and charges it to somebody else's account.

A lie protects you for a minute and costs someone you love for much longer.

3Lying gets handed down, and it comes back around

Years later, Abraham's son Isaac moved to a new place, got afraid, and told the exact same lie about his own wife. Word for word. Nobody taught him to. He just watched his dad, and it went in.

Then Isaac's son Jacob took it further. He put on a disguise and tricked his blind father to steal his brother's blessing. And here's the part that lands: years later Jacob's own sons tricked him with a bloody coat and let him believe his favorite son was dead. Jacob fooled his father with a goat. His sons fooled him with goat's blood. The preacher's line was "we reap what we sow."

So lying is not a private thing. It becomes a family habit, and it comes back to the person who started it. That's true for you too. How you handle the truth, somebody younger is watching. A little brother, a cousin, a teammate. Eventually your own kids will be.

What you don't deal with, you hand down.

4Somebody has to say "it stops with me"

The family story turns around with one brother, Judah. He had been part of the lie about Joseph. He had every reason to stay the way he was. But when he got a second chance to throw a younger brother under the bus, he stepped forward and said, "Take me instead." The pattern stopped with him.

Your chances to be Judah are small and they come every day. You broke something and nobody saw. You didn't do the thing you said you did. You know the truth will cost you something. In that moment the lie is right there, easy, and the truth is harder. The person who picks the truth anyway is the one who breaks the pattern.

You don't have to pass on what was passed to you.

5The opposite of lying isn't just "not lying"

Here is the deepest part of the story. After the hardest day of their lives on the mountain, Abraham and Isaac never talked about it. The Bible never shows them speaking again. The preacher said that silence is what did the damage.

Lying and going quiet are cousins. Both hide what's really going on. Joseph, on the other hand, became one of the most powerful men in Egypt and still cried out loud, told his brothers exactly what they had done to him, and then talked with them. The preacher said "suck it up" is not in Joseph's story.

So the goal isn't only to stop telling lies. It's to be someone who can say the hard true thing out loud: I did it. I'm scared. I need help. I'm sorry. Your parents can hear all of those. So can a coach, a mentor, or whoever gave you this link. That's the kind of person people end up trusting with everything.

Naming it is the first step to fixing it.

And one more thing, about God

The sermon was called "Promise Keeper." Abraham lied. Isaac lied. Jacob was a liar most of his life. And God kept every promise to all three of them anyway. Not because the lying didn't matter. Because God's promises were bigger than their worst moments, and God stayed with them while they were still messed up.

Jacob came out of his hardest night with a new name and a limp. The limp never went away. The things you've done leave a mark, and that's honest. But the mark is not the end of your story, and you are never in it alone.
